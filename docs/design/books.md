# 3. Books

Only sketched so far; nothing decided.

- **Proposed**: a book is a selection of posts (by date, category, tag or trip) with an order and sections (month, stage), stored as an ordered `contains` relation.
- **Proposed**: `book` and `cover` are modes of domain 2, each with its own target templates. Each post is rendered in the `print`/`book` mode. A photo can then be attached to a post for the web only, for print only, or for both, through statements about the `mode`.
- **PDF production, proposed order**:
  1. A single "book" page: all posts concatenated with a table of contents, CSS `@page` rules and page breaks, turned into a PDF by the browser or a paged-media tool. This needs no external binary and keeps the "no `shell_exec`" rule used in `wp-i18nly`.
  2. Later, optionally, server-side rendering (headless Chrome or a service) to produce PDFs automatically.
- Final assembly (cover, continuous pagination, binding margins) could be done in PHP with a library or stay in an external tool.

Format today: A5, printed at coollibri.com (a detail for now; page size must stay configurable per book).

## The manual finishing chain (from Eric's how-to notes)

Eric shared a short LibreOffice document ("howto", November 2025) describing how the first book, six months on the Camino de Santiago, was finished. It supersedes the earlier mention of PDF Arranger for the final assembly. The steps, all command-line except the table of contents and index:

1. **Merge.** The monthly PDFs are merged with `pdfcpu merge`. Each monthly file is named `<start-date>_<end-date>_<title>.pdf` (for example `20250404_20250503_compostelle_1er_mois.pdf`). A book is therefore a sequence of **parts** defined by a date range and a title, each part printed to one PDF from the browser.
2. **Resize to A5.** `pdfcpu resize "formsize:A5"`. The pages are therefore *not* printed at A5 from the browser: the layout is designed at another size and scaled down afterwards (A4 to A5 is a factor of about 0.71), so the fonts and the layout shrink with it.
3. **Reduce the photos.** Ghostscript (`gs -sDEVICE=pdfwrite -dPDFSETTINGS=/printer ...`) downsamples the images to shrink the file, presumably for the printer's upload limit.
4. **Table of contents and index.** Built in a spreadsheet, exported as tab-separated CSV, inserted into Writer ("Insert / Text from File") and formatted with styles; the result becomes part of the PDF (the intermediate file is called `compostelle_wip.pdf`).
5. **Page numbers.** Stamped on the final PDF with `pdfcpu stamp add -mode text -- "%p" ...` (bottom centre, small grey label with a rounded border). The numbers are the **physical page numbers of the PDF**, front matter included, not CSS counters. This is why the table of contents and index could be computed from the position of each post.

Consequences for the design (**proposed**):

- Everything after the browser step is command-line and automatable. A **finishing pipeline** with replaceable steps (merge, resize or scale to the book format, reduce images, stamp folios) fits behind the renderer interface; a sidecar could ship a Chromium together with `pdfcpu` and Ghostscript, which are Eric's current tools.
- Stamping folios on the final PDF is robust: it does not depend on CSS page-margin support, and the physical page number matches what the table of contents and index cite. Roman numerals for the front matter would need stamping a page range (to verify in `pdfcpu`).
- The book format should be set where the page is laid out (`@page` size in the book template) rather than by scaling afterwards; the scaling step can stay as a compatibility option.
- A part is defined by a date range and a title: composing a book from "a period", as Eric did with database queries, should be a first-class way to build the list of posts, with parts as sections of the ordered `contains` statements.
- Eric's caveat: this chain describes how the **first book** was made; it is a record of needs and constraints, not necessarily what he wants to reproduce.
- Answers: the pages were printed from the browser at **A4** (then scaled to A5); in the first book the **table of contents is at the beginning and the index at the end**; the index was formatted in Writer (ODT) and is too large to share.

Data from the table of contents of the first book (a spreadsheet of 215 rows: title and page, analyzed on 2026-10-09):

- Six parts ("Premier mois" to "Sixième mois") starting on pages 8, 42, 79, 116, 153 and 190; the last entries are "Carte" (page 230) and "Index" (page 231). The body starts on physical page 8, so seven pages precede it (cover, title and the table of contents).
- Entries are post titles (not dates). Besides the daily posts, each part has recurring sections: an opener (the month), often a "Résumé", and at the end a "whoBIRD" section (bird detections) and a "Carte" (map). Other repeated titles: "Le camino" (4 times), "Gastronomie".
- **207 of 214 units take exactly one page.** Seven take more: five of two pages and two of three pages, almost all of them "whoBIRD" sections (their length depends on the number of detections), plus one "Gastronomie". With a default of one page per unit, only a handful of exceptions would have to be declared or measured, and they belong to a recognizable kind of content.
- The table of contents has about 5 pages at the front, and **its length does not depend on the page numbers it contains** (digits do not change the number of lines): rendering it once with placeholder numbers gives its page count, then the real numbers can be filled in. With the table of contents at the front, two steps are enough; the index at the end shifts nothing.
- The structure of a part is regular: opener, optional summary, daily posts, whoBIRD, map. This suggests a **kind** on each unit of the ordered `contains` statements (opener, summary, day, appendix...).

## Table of contents and index

Eric builds both by hand today and finds them useful features; the index "needs real thinking". **Proposed**, nothing decided.

- **The core difficulty is page numbers.** They exist only after pagination, which happens in the renderer, not in WordPress. A table of contents or index *with page numbers* therefore needs a renderer that can resolve them: a paged-media polyfill such as Paged.js (`target-counter()`), or a two-pass process (render, read the page of each anchor, inject the numbers, render again). Plain browser printing cannot do it. A table of contents *without* page numbers (sections, post titles, dates) is independent of the layout and can come first.
- **Table of contents.** Derived from the structure of the book: sections and ordered posts (the ordered `contains` statements and the statements about them that give their section). The simpler of the two features.
- **Where index entries come from** (three sources, which can be combined):
  1. **Taxonomy terms** (tags, categories such as places or species): each term used by the posts of the book becomes an entry, with the posts as locators. Automatic, but coarse.
  2. **Explicit marks in the text**: an inline "index entry" mark with an optional sub-entry, a sort key and a locator anchored in the passage (the idea of `\index` in LaTeX or `indexterm` in DocBook). Precise, but costly to author by hand; the step that generates the post HTML could also emit the marks.
  3. **Statements**: an entry is the object of a statement such as `(post:12, mentions, term:45)`; sub-entries come from a "broader" relation between terms, "see also" from a relation between entries. Statements about the mention carry what an index needs: the passage anchor, whether the mention is main or passing (bold page number), and the mode (print only). This ties the index to the triples domain.
- **What a real index needs:** entries and sub-entries; "see" and "see also"; sorting that follows the language (accents, ignored leading articles, explicit sort keys; PHP's `intl` `Collator` is optional, so availability is to verify); letter headings; page ranges collapsed (12-14); main references highlighted; display forms that differ from the sort form.
- **Eric's experience (one book printed so far).** He checked by hand that each daily post fits on one page. Database queries then extracted a CSV (without page numbers) of the index entries, and the page numbers were computed and added in a second step, since a post's page follows from its position. He indexed mainly place names, then itinerary names, and, occasionally, notable incidents. Page numbers in the table of contents and index are really useful if they can be included.
- **Page numbers without a layout engine (proposed).** Eric's trick generalizes: if every post of a book starts on a new page (CSS page break before each post) and each post declares how many pages it takes (default 1, a statement about the `contains` statement), then the first page of each post is the cumulative sum of the previous page counts plus the front matter. No renderer is needed. A verification step must catch a post that overflows its declared count: the renderer, when present, reports the real page count of each post and the plugin warns on a mismatch; without a renderer, the print preview is the check. Blank pages needed so that sections start on a right-hand page can be modeled the same way.
- **Page resolution as a replaceable strategy (proposed).** Two strategies behind one interface, so that the table of contents and the index do not care which one is used: *declared* (the counting above, no dependency, post-level locators) and *measured* (Paged.js or a two-pass render, which handles posts of arbitrary length and passage-level anchors).
- **What gets indexed, and where it lives today (Eric).** Itineraries (GR10, Via Tolosana...), countries, regions and incidents are all **tags** (`post_tag`). In the printed book an itinerary tag only says that the route was followed or crossed that day, so a route is *not* a richer entity (an earlier idea of stages `part-of` a route is dropped). Cities and villages were typed in by hand, not tagged. The printed book has a single combined index.
- **Consequences for the index (proposed).**
  - Entries come from **index sources**, each one a taxonomy (any taxonomy, not only tags). The first sources are taxonomy terms; statements and inline marks come later.
  - Cities and villages: a non-public **hierarchical taxonomy** (country, region, place) used from the normal editor box, with autocomplete on names already entered, is the cheapest way to capture them at writing time; its hierarchy gives sub-entries for free. A migration helper could fill it from existing posts.
  - Tags are not hierarchical and mix several meanings, so each term needs a **kind** (place, route, incident) to be indexed and presented, and terms with no kind stay out of the index. The kind can be a statement `(term:45, has-kind, kind:route)` or term meta. If country > region sub-entries matter for tags, a "broader" statement between terms provides them.
  - One combined index is the default; the kind can drive typography or a per-kind index later.
  - **Eric's answers (to be refined later):** the selection of tags for the first book's index was done by hand, and the types of tags (place, route, incident) were told apart by **typography** in the formatted index, not by data. A flat index was enough (no country > region > city hierarchy). Cities and villages could simply be added as tags too, which avoids a dedicated taxonomy. So the kind of a term and its inclusion in the index become explicit data (a term with no kind is excluded) and typography follows from the kind; a hierarchy stays an option.
- **Parts of variable length (Eric's objection).** The table of contents, the index and chapter openings have no fixed page count, so declaring counts for them by hand would burden the user. Proposed ways to remove most of that burden:
  - **Numbering design (optional, no longer needed).** An earlier idea was roman numerals for the front matter and the table of contents after the body. Eric's first book shows it is not needed: the table of contents is at the front, its length is independent of its page numbers and is found by a first pass, and the folios are the physical pages of the PDF. Roman numerals and a table of contents at the back stay possible as layout options.
  - **Chapter openings:** give each opening a declared page count (default 1), as for posts, and **calibrate instead of asking the user to count**: a renderer measures the real count and the plugin stores it; without a renderer, a "Pages" screen lists each unit with its status (declared, verified, mismatch) and lets the user type the count seen in the print preview once. An idea to evaluate: import the produced PDF and find the page of each post title to fill the counts.
  - With a measuring renderer none of this is needed; the declared strategy is the dependency-free baseline.
- **Export.** The table of contents and the index can also be exported as CSV or JSON (with page numbers when known), which replaces Eric's database queries and keeps external steps possible.
- **Delivery order (proposed):** table of contents without page numbers first; then page numbers by the declared strategy (page break per post, page counts, overflow check); then the index (places and routes from terms and statements, incidents from marks), as its own slice after a design note; the measured strategy later, when posts longer than a page or passage-level anchors are needed.


## PDF production: automation options

**Proposed**, nothing decided.

- **Constraint.** A PHP PDF library (Dompdf, mPDF, TCPDF) is a poor fit: block themes rely on modern CSS (flex, grid, CSS variables), and the pages contain JavaScript-rendered maps (Leaflet, WP GPX Maps) and lazily loaded images. A real browser engine is needed. The plugin itself stays PHP-only (no `shell_exec`), so rendering happens outside WordPress.
- **What the plugin provides.** The `print`/`book` modes and their templates; a **single book page** (all posts of a book in order, table of contents, `@page` size and margins, continuous page numbers); a **manifest** of the book (ordered list of URLs with mode and options) through the REST API and WP-CLI.
- **What an external renderer does.** Opens the book page, or each URL of the manifest, in headless Chromium (for example Playwright, `page.pdf()` with header and footer disabled and the CSS page size preferred), waits for maps and images to finish loading, and merges the PDFs if there are several. With one book page, page numbering, table of contents and running headers become continuous, which per-post PDFs merged afterwards cannot give.
- **Eric's preference:** ideally on the server, but that means installing extra software, which could put off other users of the plugin. His own site runs under Docker, so it is not a problem for him. Serving the assembled result as HTML + CSS and printing from a browser (Samsung Internet while it adds no header, or a small dedicated Android app) is also considered.
- **Renderer backends** (**proposed** design): optional and pluggable behind one interface, all consuming the same book page and manifest.
  1. **Browser, the default, no dependency.** The plugin serves the book as HTML + CSS and the user prints it from a browser. `@page { margin: 0 }` with padding on the content normally hides the browser's own header and footer in Chromium-based browsers (to verify on Samsung Internet), which would remove the dependence on one browser. A small Android app (a WebView and the Android print framework) is possible but is a separate project and is not planned.
  2. **Sidecar HTTP renderer, optional.** A headless Chromium service in a container next to WordPress, called over HTTP by the plugin: no `shell_exec`, nothing installed in the WordPress container. It fits Eric's Docker setup and stays an opt-in for other users. Gotenberg is a known candidate (HTML or URL to PDF, and PDF merging); its current API and how to wait for the maps are to verify. The service must be able to reach the site, and the plugin only ever sends URLs of its own site.
  3. **External tool consuming the manifest, optional:** a command-line tool on a laptop or in CI.
- **Limitation to check:** Chromium does not support the paged-media features needed for a table of contents with page numbers and running headers (`target-counter()`, running elements). Paged.js polyfills them in the browser; a renderer based on Chromium shares the limitation unless it also runs such a polyfill.
- **Print link in the views** (**decided**): a block that builds the link to the current page in a given mode, shown only when a relation exists for the current template and that mode, replacing hand-written links in the templates.
- **Open for Books:** whether any renderer backend lives in this project or in a separate tool, and how far "Books" goes beyond composing the book and exposing the manifest.

### One possible approach: a Gotenberg sidecar, one PDF per post, merged, then numbered

**Proposed**, one solution among others; nothing decided and nothing tried yet (Eric, 2026-10-22: he had not known Gotenberg and was going to try it by hand). What follows comes from the documentation as remembered, **not verified**.

- **The service.** A [Gotenberg](https://gotenberg.dev/) container (headless Chromium behind an HTTP API) next to WordPress; it can convert a URL to PDF with the paper size, the margins, a wait for the page to finish loading, and can merge PDFs. It is the "sidecar HTTP renderer" above. A first trial needs only `docker run --rm -p 3000:3000 gotenberg/gotenberg:8` and a `curl` on `/forms/chromium/convert/url` (sizes in inches).
- **One PDF per post, not one HTML page for the whole book.** Each post is converted from **its own address** in `?mode=print`, which is the page Eric already sees and validates in the preview panel (slice 219). A single HTML page for the whole book was considered and judged risky for this blog: posts use **different templates** (day, monthly summary…), each with its own header, blocks and QR code, so merging them into one document means rebuilding each post outside its template or cutting pieces out of rendered HTML; several Leaflet maps and galleries in one document collide (identifiers, scripts); hundreds of pages cost memory and time and one error loses the whole book; and the page validated in the preview would not be the page printed. Per-post conversion fails post by post and can resume.
- **The page format is the same setting as the preview**: width, height and margins of **Settings → Otherguise books** feed the paper size and the margins of the conversion.
- **Page numbers.** Gotenberg can fill an HTML footer or header in which the elements with the classes `pageNumber`, `totalPages`, `date`, `title` and `url` are replaced by Chromium. That numbers **one conversion**: each post restarts at page 1. Continuous numbers for the merged book would be stamped **afterwards**, by a finishing step (the "finishing pipeline" and its "stamp folios" step above). Whether Gotenberg itself can stamp numbers on a merged PDF is to find out; if not, a command-line PDF tool does it.
- **Table of contents with page numbers.** Count the pages of each post's PDF (which also gives the real "fits on one page?" that the preview can only estimate) and store the count; the numbers of the table of contents are the sums. This is the *declared* strategy above with measured counts. Open: the table of contents is itself made of pages, so it needs a fixed number of pages or two passes.
- **Fallback between the two:** one HTML page per part (a month, for instance) instead of per post or per book, if stamping or merging proves a problem. No reason to start there.

**Calling the service from PHP: a small client of our own rather than the Gotenberg PHP library (Proposed; the library was not examined, what follows is from memory).** The Gotenberg client library for PHP (`gotenberg/gotenberg-php`, installed with Composer) would give a readable builder (URL, paper size, margins, wait, footer, merge) and the multipart upload of files such as `footer.html`, and would follow the API versions of Gotenberg. Its cost in a WordPress plugin: it relies on PSR-7 and PSR-18 and on an HTTP client (Guzzle or Symfony), which can collide with the same packages loaded by another plugin in another version, unless they are prefixed (Strauss or PHP-Scoper), a build step this plugin does not have (it has no `vendor/` today); it adds a second HTTP client next to `wp_remote_request()` (timeouts, proxy, certificates, filters), and timeouts matter for long conversions; and tests would need a fake PSR-18 client where the WordPress HTTP API can be faked with the filter `pre_http_request`. So the orientation is a **small client of our own in the `Books` module**: `wp_remote_request()` with a `multipart/form-data` body (a few dozen lines: boundary, fields, files), no dependency, hidden behind the renderer interface above so that another engine stays possible, and sending only URLs of the site. The library stays a **reference** for parameter names and for the cases it handles; it can be reconsidered, with prefixing, if our client grows (several routes, API changes). Nothing decided.

**To examine: the "PDF engines" of Gotenberg.** Besides the Chromium conversion, Gotenberg has a family of PDF operations (merge, split, flatten, convert to PDF/A and PDF/UA, read and write metadata, embed files, and possibly watermark or stamp, encrypt…). Which of these exist in the current version, with which parameters, and whether any of them numbers the pages, is to be read in its documentation and tried: it decides how much of the finishing pipeline Gotenberg covers and what must be added.

### Resolution and weight of the images in the PDF (analysis of 2026-10-20; Proposed, nothing built)

**Why it matters.** The printer asks for **300 ppi at least**. Last year Eric had to reduce the PDF files because the photos were far above that. Four PDF files were analysed with `pdfimages -list` (poppler); the ppi of an image is its pixels divided by the size at which **this PDF** places it, so the figures below are **for the page format and the layout of each file (A4 with margins of 10 mm)**, not a property of the photo.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rabbi, printed by Firefox | cairo (Firefox) | 4.25 MB | 1026 x 581, 152 ppi, **lossless** (Flate), 1.5 MB | two at 455 ppi, 1.07 and 1.47 MB, lossless | map tiles 96 ppi |
| Flysch, Gotenberg, 2026-10-20 | Skia (Chromium 155) | 1.0 MB | 1024 x 577 **JPEG**, 152 ppi, 230 KB | 300 x 169 JPEG, **133 ppi**, 35 to 69 KB each | map tiles 96 ppi |
| Cap de Peñas, Gotenberg, 2026-10-20 | Skia (Chromium 155) | 0.79 MB | 1024 x 577 JPEG, 152 ppi, 173 KB | 300 x 169 JPEG, 133 ppi | map tiles 96 ppi |
| Cap de Peñas, printed on a phone, July 2025 (the one that printed well) | Skia (Chromium 130) | 3.88 MB | the same, 152 ppi | 1024 x 573 or 577, **455 ppi**: six JPEG (132 to 192 KB) and **two lossless images of 1.36 and 1.17 MB** | altitude profile 679 x 525 at 336 ppi, map tiles 96 ppi |

| **The page sent to the printer** (Cap de Peñas, extracted from the book with PDF Arranger, A5, November 2025; the result was **very good**) | pdfcpu (PDF 1.3) | **0.57 MB** | 1024 x 577 JPEG, **216 ppi**, 173 KB | 477 x 267 or 269 JPEG, **300 ppi**, 25 to 52 KB each | 427 x 330, 299 ppi | tiles recompressed as JPEG, 136 ppi |

The three files of the same article (the phone's, today's Gotenberg and the printer's) are kept in [`samples/cap-de-penas/`](samples/cap-de-penas/README.md).

What it shows:

- **The page of the printer is the A4 page scaled by 0.707, not a layout made for A5**: its proportions and margins are those of the A4 files (checked by rendering both side by side), and the main photo, which is 171 mm wide on the A4 page, is 120 mm on the A5 page (1024 px at 216 ppi). So the sequence of last year's book was: A4 page, then scaling to A5, then compression and page number. **The target resolution is the one at the final size**: 300 ppi on A5 is 300 x 0.707 = **212 ppi at the A4 generation**. The calculation below (2020 px and 680 px) was for 300 ppi *on the A4 page* and is 1.41 times too much for a book printed in A5: about **1420 px** for the main photo and **480 px** for a gallery thumbnail (477 px are indeed in the file of the printer). To be confirmed with Eric: A4 scaled to A5 again, or a layout made for A5?
- **The printer accepted images below 300 ppi**: the main photo at 216 ppi (the file only has 1024 px, so the compression could not raise it) and the map tiles at 136 ppi. The practical requirement is therefore not "300 ppi everywhere" (what the printer really checks is to be asked).
- **The compression step reduced the gallery to 300 ppi at the final size** (from the 1024 px variants to 477 px, JPEG of 25 to 52 KB each): this is what an early reduction in the workflow would have to produce directly.
- **Why last year's phone file had a higher resolution than today's: the pixel ratio of the screen.** A browser picks the variant of an image in the `srcset` by the **device pixel ratio**: the phone, with a ratio of 3 or more, took the 1024 px variants (455 ppi) and drew the **altitude profile** (a canvas of the chart plugin, which uses the pixel ratio) at 679 x 525 (336 ppi), while Chromium of Gotenberg works at a ratio of **1** (194 x 150, 96 ppi; its documentation has no option for the pixel ratio). That is also why the 300 px thumbnails were chosen. Two consequences: the variants have to be asked by name (below), and the profile of the altitude would need a higher pixel ratio for the chart in the print mode (for example setting the option `devicePixelRatio` of the chart, or `window.devicePixelRatio` before it is built; **not tried**). The map tiles stay at 96 ppi: raster tiles of 256 px, unless a service offers tiles at twice the resolution.
- **Fonts and PDF details of the printer's file**: the fonts are embedded except `Helvetica` (the number of the page, added afterwards by the tool, not embedded), the PDF is 1.3 (pdfcpu), without a tagged structure; the file has no bleed or crop marks (the five boxes are the page).

- **Chromium (so Gotenberg) copies a JPEG into the PDF as it is** (checked with a test: a JPEG of 842 KB gave a PDF of 845 KB, `enc: jpeg`); it neither re-encodes nor reduces the resolution. Firefox (cairo) decodes and stores **without loss**, which is why its file is four times heavier for the same pixels. The weight of a PDF of Gotenberg is the sum of the JPEG files that the browser chose.
- **The resolution is decided by the variant of the image that the browser picks in the `srcset`**, at one device pixel per CSS pixel, so about the width of the slot on the screen (96 to 150 ppi), not by the original photo. Today the gallery uses the size **300 x 169** (133 ppi) and the main photo the size **1024** (152 ppi): **both under 300 ppi**. Last year's good print used the 1024 px variant for the gallery (455 ppi).
- **The weight of last year's file came mostly from two images**, not from the resolution: without the two lossless images of 1.36 and 1.17 MB it would weigh about 1.3 MB. Why Chromium could not copy those two as JPEG is **not known**: they are 1024 x **573** while the others are 1024 x **577** (another ratio, so another route of making the variant), and the pixels cannot tell more. Eric's remark: the phone that took these photos made **"motion" photos**, which weigh much more with no gain of resolution; the variants made by the WordPress image editor are re-encoded, which drops what is added to the file, but the originals keep it.
- **The estimate of a first message was too pessimistic**: scaling the weight by the number of pixels gave about 3.5 MB for the flysch page at 300 ppi. The thumbnails of 300 px are very lightly compressed (about 10 bits per pixel) while the variants of 1024 px weigh 130 to 190 KB, so eight photos of about 700 px should weigh of the order of 1 MB. To be measured.
- **The needed pixels depend on the printed size**, so on the page format and the layout: on A4 the main photo is placed at about 6.7 in and a thumbnail at 2.3 in (hence about 2020 px and 680 px for 300 ppi *on the A4 page*, see above for the final size); on A5 the same layout is narrower (usable width 128 mm against 190 mm) and the same files would print at about 1.5 times the ppi. Compute for the format of the book, not once for all.
- **The map tiles are 96 ppi** (raster tiles of the screen): soft on paper whatever is done to the photos. The altitude profile was at 336 ppi last year and is at 96 ppi in today's file (to look into).

**What Media Helper does for the sizes** (code of `wp-media-helper` read on 2026-10-20: `Thumbnails/Thumbnails.php`, `Blocks/GalleryBlock.php`):

- The sizes are the **named image sizes registered in WordPress** (`wp_get_registered_image_subsizes()`: thumbnail, medium, large…, and any size added with `add_image_size`). The size `300 x 169` seen in the PDF is the **named size `gallery`**, which is the **default of the attribute `size` of the block `wp-media-helper/gallery`** (a cropped 16:9 size).
- For an attachment registered by Media Helper, a **named size that does not exist is created when it is asked for** (in the filter `image_downsize`), written in a cache directory of its own, and the `srcset` points to that cache. The registered sizes are also made **in the background after the registration** by a cron event, but Media Helper **assumes that there is no cron when `DISABLE_WP_CRON` is set** (`schedule()` starts from `! DISABLE_WP_CRON` and applies the filter **`wp_media_helper_background_thumbnails`**): on Eric's sites the constant is set **and a system cron runs WP-Cron**, so by default nothing is scheduled although it could be; a site with a system cron turns the background generation on with that filter (`add_filter( 'wp_media_helper_background_thumbnails', '__return_true' )`). Without it, the sizes are made at the first request that asks for them. (Corrected on 2026-10-21: a first version of this note said that the background generation could not work on Eric's sites.) A size registered **only in the print mode** is not known when an attachment is registered, so it is never made in the background anyway: it has to be made by the first print render, by a step before the conversion (the PDF job can ask for the sizes of the images of the post before calling the server: it runs in the cron, with no page time limit) or by a WP-CLI command that generates them for the posts of a book.
- The resize is made with the **WordPress image editor** (GD or Imagick), with the orientation of the photo applied to the copy only, never to the original; a size is never larger than the original (WordPress falls back to the full image); a limit of pixels and of memory protects the page view.
- The block takes the **`size` as an attribute**, so a print template can ask for another named size without any code in a plugin.

**Orientation (Proposed), revised on 2026-10-21 with Eric's answers.**

1. **The theme knows names, Books owns the dimensions** (Proposed). The print templates of the theme use **semantic names of image sizes** (the `size` attribute of the block `wp-media-helper/gallery`, the `sizeSlug` of the image blocks) that say the place taken: full width, a half, a third, a third cropped 16:9. **Books registers them with `add_image_size()`** and computes the pixels from its own setting of the page format: usable width (page minus margins) x fraction x 300 ppi. The theme declares only the **fractions and the ratios** of its layout (for example by a filter of Books), not pixels: changing the page, A4 or A5, changes the dimensions without touching the theme. For an A5 page of 148 mm with margins of 14 mm that is about 1420 px for the full width and 480 px for a thumbnail of about 40 mm (the figures of the file of the printer).
2. **Three traps to deal with** (found in the code of WordPress and Media Helper, **not tried**):
   - **A size that exists is not read again.** For a name that an attachment already has, Media Helper keeps the entry of the metadata without comparing its dimensions with the definition: a name redefined with other pixels does nothing for the photos that already went through it. Either Media Helper treats a size whose dimensions differ from the current definition as **missing**, or the name carries the pixels (`books-print-480x270`) and Books translates the semantic name into the real one at render time (heavier).
   - **The `srcset` can defeat the choice.** WordPress puts in the `srcset` every size with the same ratio as the one asked; for a thumbnail 16:9 of 480 px Chromium at a pixel ratio of 1 takes the **smallest candidate that covers the slot** (the 300 px of the `gallery` size, which is still there). In the print mode the `srcset` has to be removed so that the named size is the only one (to be checked by trying).

   - **Photos that Media Helper does not own (found on testing, 2026-10-20; answered by Media Helper the same day, `main` of `evlist/wp-media-helper`, unit tests only).** The photos of last year (for example attachment 41025, `photos/2025/eric/07/03/…`) have no `_wp_media_helper_source_id`: Media Helper did not create their sizes (WordPress fell back to the original, a PDF of 27.5 MB) and `thumbnails generate` said "0 would need something". ## The definition of a book: sections, units and kinds (brainstorming, Eric and Claude, 2026-10-23)

Everything in this section is **Proposed**; nothing is decided or coded. It builds on the sections above (the first book's chain, the table of contents and the index, the declared and measured page strategies) and on the example "Assemble a book" of [`usage-examples.md`](usage-examples.md) (`books/contains`, ordered, qualified by `books/pages`).

### Three notions

1. **The section (the structure).** One recursive entity: a book, a volume, a chapter or a month are the same thing at different depths. It holds an **ordered list of units**. A section can be in several books (`subjects_of( $section, 'books/contains' )`).
2. **The unit (the role in the book).** A post or page, or another section, with a **kind**: cover, endpaper, title page, chapter opener, summary, day, appendix, map, table of contents, index… The kind is a **role in this book, not a property of the content**: Eric's monthly article is an ordinary article on the web and a chapter opener in the book, so the kind cannot be deduced from the post type. It is carried by the `contains` statement, like `books/pages` already is.
3. **The template (the form).** What the Modes module already does: the print variant of a template of the theme.

### A kind references a template and carries constraints

A kind is **neither a template nor a metadata of the template**: it **refers to** a template (a print variant) and **carries rules**. Two reasons: one template can serve two kinds (a day and a gastronomy post share the layout, not the constraints), and the constraints are about the role, not the layout. A small registry, on the model of `books_print_sizes` (slice 221): the theme declares the names of the kinds and the template to use, Books owns the rules. A kind would carry:

| Property | Examples |
|---|---|
| template (print variant) | `single-print`, a month opener, a cover |
| number of pages | exact (1 for a day), variable (opener, whoBIRD), range |
| break before | new page, new right-hand page, none |
| in the numbered sequence | yes (body), no (cover) |
| in the table of contents | yes/no, level |
| generated | table of contents, index, map (no post behind) |

The kinds of the first book already give a list: cover, endpaper, table of contents, month opener (several pages), summary, day (one page), whoBIRD (1 to 3 pages), map, index (see the data above: 207 of 214 units take exactly one page).

### The whole chain

1. **Resolve** the section into a flat ordered list of units (the generated kinds have no post).
2. **Render** one PDF per unit (slice 220), with the cache.
3. **Measure** the real page count of each PDF (Gotenberg's metadata, already used by the panel).
4. **Check** it against the rule of the kind and report the differences (the case of 2026-10-21: an empty paragraph gave 2 pages instead of 1; report by how much the content exceeds the page, not only "2 pages").
5. **Number**: cumulative sum of the page counts (the declared strategy), or the measured one.
6. **Generate** the table of contents (rendered once with placeholder numbers to know its length, then for real) and the index.
7. **Merge and stamp** the folios.

### Points to keep in mind

- **The cover is outside the numbered sequence.** With many print-on-demand printers it is a separate file whose spine depends on the page count. **To verify with the printer (coollibri); not checked.**
- **Blank pages**: a chapter that must start on a right-hand page may need an inserted empty page; whether the printer wants an even total or a multiple of four is **unknown, to verify**.
- **A fixed page count is a rule, a variable one is a measure**: a variable kind (whoBIRD, opener) is measured once and the count stored. This is the "target number of pages per page" idea: it comes from the kind, with a default, and a unit can override it.

### Possible order of construction, smallest first

1. **The kinds and their page rule**, with the report of overflows in the preview panel (the PDF already gives its page count): useful before any book exists.
2. **The section**: its ordered list of units and the merge into one PDF.
3. **Numbering and the table of contents.**
4. **The generated kinds** (index, map), then the cover.

### Open choices (Eric)

- **Where the kind of a unit lives**: on the `contains` statement (per book, more flexible) or as a default of the post, overridden per book (simpler). Claude leans to the second.
- **Who declares the kinds**: the theme through a filter (as for the image sizes), or a settings screen. Claude leans to the theme: a site rarely changes its kinds, and the theme is where the templates already change.

### The name (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-23; Proposed)

The generic entity is called a **section** (French: *section*), not "part": "part" is already taken (`template_part`, `modes/has-part-variant`, "part variant"), and in the first book "parts" were the six months. "Section" was first set aside because Eric's vocabulary already used it for a month or a stage; that is in fact the same thing (a month is a section of the book, a chapter a section that holds sections), so the word fits as a generic, recursive term. "Division" (TEI) was considered and is less natural in French and in English outside TEI. The identifier would be `book_section` if it ever becomes a post type (12 characters, under the limit of 20), and the code should prefix its names (`Books\…`) to avoid confusion with the settings sections of WordPress (`add_settings_section`, already used by the settings screen of Books). A book, a volume, a chapter and a month are **kinds of sections**; a section that no other section contains is, in fact, a book. Not checked: whether the site editor of WordPress uses "section" in another sense.


### A section is a post, not a new post type (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-23; Proposed)

- **Idea (Eric).** A section is not necessarily a new type of content: it can be the extension of an existing **page or article**, which then holds other posts. Eric's example: a section for a van trip in the Pyrenees, which will have a cover, an introduction, a general account, photos and "day" articles. Being a post, it has a **web version and a print version** through the modes, like any other post.
- **What it gives.** A title, text, excerpt, featured image and the editor for free. **The text of the section is its own opener**: last year the article of the month was the opener of the chapter, here the general account of the trip is the opener of the section, so no kind "opener" is needed apart from the section itself. `books/contains` already relates a post to a post whatever their types (`usage-examples.md`, "Assemble a book"); a section would usually be a **page** (no date, outside the flow of the blog), the days staying articles. A day can be in several sections, which the hierarchy of WordPress pages would not allow.
- **Two renderings to tell apart.** (1) The **own content** of the section, in web or print mode, like a page: this is what the modes already do. (2) The **assembly** of the book: the opener followed by the units in order, merged into one PDF: the work of Books, later.
- **The cover** could be the same post rendered in a **mode `cover`** with its own template (featured image, title). **To check with the Modes module**, not looked at.
- **Consequences.** There is no menu for "all my sections": they are the posts that have at least one `books/contains` statement (a query on the relations; a filter on the list of pages could be added). Any post is a section as soon as it contains something; whether it is public is the ordinary status of the post (draft or private while preparing).
- **A first usable tool (proposed, not planned).** A panel in the editor, next to the print preview: in a day, "In the section: [choose]" with add and remove; in a section, the ordered list of its contents with reordering and removal; the default kind of a unit is "day". Eric could attach the days as he prepares them, before any assembly exists. Planned in detail: [slice 223](../slices/223-books-section-container.md).

### Previewing and converting unpublished posts (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-23; Proposed, revised the same day)

- **The problem (Eric).** The PDF conversion of slice 220 refuses a post that is not published (decision 1 of slice 219): Gotenberg fetches the page like an anonymous visitor and WordPress shows a draft only to a logged-in user who may edit it. A section that is being prepared, with days that may still be drafts, could not be converted. Eric's idea: **a link with a random code in the URL**, as Nextcloud does for sharing.
- **Revised after Eric's remark.** A first proposal minted a short-lived, hashed token for each conversion job. Eric observed that many sites share a private page by a random token in the URL (Nextcloud), that the data here is **of little sensitivity** (the worst case is that someone sees a section before it is finished), and that the same mechanism is useful **outside the PDF** to share a private page, so a token that is **permanent until revoked** is acceptable. That is simpler: **one token per post**, created on demand, kept in clear (a shared link must be shown again), revoked or regenerated by the user. The conversion just reads it.
- **A token on a section covers its contents**, like the share of a folder: the link of a section opens the section and its units (drafts included), so an assembly needs one address and a section can be proofread by someone before it is published. Shares itself does not know sections: the module that does adds them through a filter.
- **It is not specific to Books**, since it serves any mode and any private page: a generic feature, planned as its own module **Shares**, which Books uses when it is there (as it uses the modes). Planned in detail in [slice 400](../slices/400-shares-private-link.md).
- **Precautions kept** (the risk is judged low, not nil): `noindex`, no cache, no referrer on the responses served through a link; the token never in the REST API; skipped from the WordPress export; the link opens only that post (or that section and its units), read-only; a revoked or regenerated token stops working at once; the URL appears in the access logs and the browser history, which is documented.
- **Considered and not retained.** Sending the **login cookie** of the user to Gotenberg (`cookies` option): it hands a session to another service. Rendering the page in WordPress and sending the **HTML** to Gotenberg (no fetch of the page): the scripts of the maps, the chart and the QR code, and the relative addresses of the assets, would run from a document without the origin of the site (not tried).
- **Not covered.** Unsaved changes of the editor (decision 12 of slice 219): the link shows what is **saved**.
- **Open.** Whether an assembly of a section **includes** its unpublished units (Eric needs it while preparing; a published book would not).
- **Decided (Eric, 2026-10-25), after a try of slice 400 on testing: the link with a token is for preview and page counting, not for the final printed version.** `?shared=<token>&mode=print` works for Gotenberg, but the QR code carries `?p=<id>` while the post is not published (no final address yet), shorter than the final one, so its size and the page count differ and the printed code would be wrong. The PDF to print is made from a published post.

### Roles, a loop block, unlisted posts, and the templates as the plan of the book (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-25; Proposed)

Brainstorming; nothing is decided or coded. It continues "The definition of a book: sections, units and kinds" above.

**1. A role for the posts.** Cover (front and back), title page, content, table of contents, index… A role is the **kind** of a unit of the sections above, given here **to the post** as a default, which a section can override (the lean already recorded). A registry of roles declared by the theme or the site, like the modes, each with its rules (pages, page break, in the table of contents).

**2. A block that loops over the posts of a section.** To put in a post or in a template of a section, to loop over the included posts, filtered by role, in the order of the section (dates and positions). Candidate route, **to verify with a short experiment before anything is written**: a *variation* of the core Query Loop block (`core/query`) whose query is replaced, through the filter `query_loop_block_query_vars`, by the ordered list of the section (`post__in` and `orderby=post__in`), so that the blocks of a line (title, image, excerpt, content) are the core ones. Consequences:
- a section **with or without content of its own**: the template has the block of the content or not;
- the **order of assembly is the order of the blocks of the template**, which is in the theme: the index before or after the content, the cover first, a title page, and so on. Several editions are several templates.

**3. Different templates in web and in print** (the variants of the Modes module). For the same loop: in the web mode **links** to the pages (or sub-sections), or their summaries; in the print mode either the pages **included**, or **nothing visible**, because the pages are printed separately. In that last case the loop can write **hidden data** that the production of the book uses: the plan of the book (see below), the pagination.

**4. Unlisted posts ("published but hidden").** For a cover or a title page, which is not meant to be read as a post of the blog. Two independent things: **visibility** (listed, **unlisted**, private) and **access by a token** (slice 400). *Unlisted* = the address works, but the post is absent from the home page, the archives, the feeds, the search, the sitemaps and the previous/next links, with `noindex`. Proposed: a **flag** on the post and filters on the queries (`pre_get_posts` for what is not a single post, the sitemaps, the adjacent links, the robots), not a custom status of WordPress (the block editor handles them badly). An unlisted post needs **no token** (Gotenberg reaches it by its address); the token is for drafts and private posts. Limit: a plugin that reads the database without the filters of WordPress sees them.

**5. A module without dependency, not a plugin (yet).** The token and the unlisted state need neither Triples nor Books: **one module, `Access`**, with no dependency (checked by `ArchitectureTest`), used by Books through functions and filters as Books uses the modes. Separating it into a plugin would add a unit to tag and to copy by hand on the sites and a dependency between plugins, for no gain now; the architecture already says that the modules can be split later, when a second use or another rhythm of release asks for it.

#### The templates as the plan of the book (Eric's addition and Claude's opinion)

**The idea (Eric).** The templates of a theme decide **the order in which the book is assembled** (the index before or after the content, for example). In the print mode the loop block can show **nothing visible** and write **hidden data** that the production uses, in particular for the pagination.

**Opinion (Claude): a good idea**, with these points:
- **One place for the structure.** The data (the posts of the section, their order, their roles) says *what* and *in which order within a role*; the template says *how the groups are arranged*. The theme repository is already where the templates change, and an edition is a template.
- **The plan of the book is read from the page.** The production fetches the print page of the section and reads a **manifest**: the ordered list of units (post, role, pages expected), with the places of the generated units (table of contents, index). Nothing to store apart from the statements and the templates. A section inside a section gives a nested manifest.
- **Format.** A `<script type="application/json" data-books-manifest>`, not hidden elements: it has no box and cannot move the layout. This matters since a block that looks empty is enough to push a page over (the empty paragraph of 2026-10-21, 20 px of margin); a script has no margin and cannot be printed by a wrong rule of the theme.
- **Only identifiers, not addresses or titles.** The page of a published section is public: a manifest with the addresses of unlisted posts would list them. The production works out the addresses itself.
- **Two uses of "hidden data", to keep apart**: (a) the **plan** of the book, read by the production from the page of the section; (b) **markers for the pagination inside each unit** (anchors of the headings that go in the table of contents), read from the PDF or the page of the unit. The first can also come later from a walk of the blocks of the template on the server, with the same format.
- **The cache.** The key of the PDF of a section must include its contents (the list and the order), which are not in `post_modified`.
- **Not a single HTML for the whole book.** The loop that includes the content of every post serves lists and short sections; for the book the assembly stays one PDF per unit, merged (the objection of Eric about a single HTML is still right).

**Decided (Eric, 2026-10-25), the three points left open above:**
- **Where the role is chosen**: in the panel "Book section" of the editor, with **a selector of the role and a switch "unlisted"**.
- **When the manifest is emitted**: **at every print render** of the section (not only for the production). Consequence, to keep in mind: the page of a published section in the print mode then shows, in its source, the identifiers of its units, their roles and the pages expected. That reveals nothing that the enumeration of `?p=<id>` does not (WordPress redirects it to the address of an unlisted post, and shows nothing of a draft or a private post without access), and the manifest holds no title and no address; but it is public, so it holds nothing that is not meant to be.
- **The module is called `Access`** (the token of slice 400 and the unlisted state), not `Shares`.

Still **Proposed**: everything else of this section, in particular the variation of the Query Loop block (to try first), the format of the manifest and the use of the hidden data for the pagination.

#### Blocks decide the plan; the manifest is collected from them (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-25; Proposed)

- **Who decides the list and the order.** The data (`books/contains` and the position pins) give the posts of a section and their order **within a role**; the blocks of the templates and of the posts (the loop block) read them, filter them (role, unlisted, not published, mode) and decide which groups appear and in which order. So the blocks are the **engine** of the manifest: only the rendering knows what the page really holds. A manifest worked out from the data alone could differ from what the template shows.
- **One collector, not one script per block.** Each loop block hands its units to a collector, in the order of the document; one manifest is written at the end of the render. A section inside a section gives a nested manifest. Open: how the collector knows that the render is over (a block at the end of the template, or a filter), and a block placed in the content of a post that is itself included in a loop.

#### Mixed sections: sub-sections and own content (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-25; Proposed, starting orientation agreed by Eric)

**The difficulty.** Each unit of the book is a PDF of its own, and the PDFs are merged. A post is rendered as one HTML page, one continuous flow. A section laid out as *start, sub-sections, own content, sub-sections, end* is a sequence of units, the own content being **one** unit (start and end being generated units: title page, table of contents, index). A section whose own content appears **twice** around sub-sections (*start, sub-sections, own content, sub-sections, own content, end*) would need one post to be cut into two renders.

**Consequences if it were allowed**: every cut is a hard page break (a right-hand page in a two-sided book); the page numbers cannot come from the CSS counters of a page, they are worked out when the PDFs are merged, from the number of pages of each one; running heads and heading counters must carry on; the table of contents depends on the number of pages of everything before it, so all the units must be rendered before the numbers are known; a unit of the manifest would be identified by (post, part) and no longer by the post.

**Options, from the most restrictive**
0. **Forbid mixed sections**: a section either lists sub-sections or has content of its own, not both. The most extreme; keeps every unit a whole post.
1. **One own content per section** (the **starting orientation**): a section may mix sub-sections and own content, but the content of the post appears **once** in the template. The collector refuses or warns when a template inserts it twice, with a clear message.
2. **Solve it in the plan**: the second part becomes another post or a sub-section (a "Conclusion", an "Appendix"). Every unit stays a whole post; the editorial answer to a need for 1+.
3. **A block "cut here" in the content of a post**: the post is rendered twice (`part=1`, `part=2`), each render keeping its side of the cut; one cache key per part; widows and orphans at the cut. Only if the need is real.
4. **Merge all the HTML before the pagination** (one document for the whole section, with a print engine that paginates it): it removes the problem of the cuts and the numbering, but it **reverts a founding decision** (one PDF per post, merged) and the merged document may be too large to render (memory, time, the images of the whole book at once). Not retained now; to reconsider only if the volume turns out to be manageable.

**To start with**: 1, with 2 as the way out, and 0 as a possible tightening if mixed sections turn out to be more trouble than they are worth. 3 and 4 are kept in reserve.

#### Experiment of 2026-10-25: a Query Loop that lists the units of a section, and a manifest collected from the render

Tried on a scratch WordPress 7.1.3 (Twenty Twenty-Five), with a throw-away must-use plugin (not kept, not part of the plugin): three filters and one action, about 60 lines. **Nothing of this is built yet**; this is what was seen.

- **The marker.** The namespace of a variation is **not** visible to the filter that changes the query: `query_loop_block_query_vars` receives the block `core/post-template`, whose context has the `query` attribute of the Query Loop but not its `namespace`. A key added **inside the `query` attribute** (here `books_units`) reaches it, and the editor keeps it.
- **The list and the order.** In the filter: `post__in` = the ids of the section in order (the order of `Sections::order()`, pins included), `orderby = post__in`, `post_type = any`. The loop then shows the units in the order of the panel; a pin (a unit moved to the first rank) changes the loop and the manifest. The unlisted unit (slice 402) **is shown**, since a query with `post__in` is left alone by the filters of the unlisted state: wanted for a section, to remember for the other lists.
- **The collector.** A frame is opened in `pre_render_block` and closed in `render_block` for the Query Loop that carries the marker, and every `the_post` inside it adds the post to the frame: the manifest holds **what is really rendered**, in the order of the document. Two details: `setup_postdata()` fires `the_post` too (so `wp_reset_postdata()` adds the section itself at the end of the loop), the events of the main query must be ignored; and the manifest is written in `wp_footer`, which a block theme calls, as `<script type="application/json" data-books-manifest>`.
- **Nesting.** A unit that is itself a section, shown in the loop with its content, opens a second frame: the manifest is a list of frames (`section` and its `units` with a `depth`); the nesting is rebuilt from the section ids. Not tried: the same inside a template part, or a loop inside the content of a unit that is not a section.
- **Unpublished units (slice 403, the part left for later).** When the visitor holds the **token of the section**, the filter adds `draft`, `pending`, `private` and `future` to `post_status`: the units appear in the loop and in the manifest. The link of each unpublished unit is given **its own token** by a filter on `post_link` and `page_link` (only inside the loop, and only when the visit carries the token of the section), and following that link as an anonymous visitor shows the draft. Without the token of the section: the published units only, and no token anywhere. Side effect to decide: the tokens of the units are **made while a page is displayed** (a GET); it only happens for someone who holds the token of the section.
- **Still open**: the collector as a class of Books and where it lives (a filter on the render, a block at the end of the template); what the manifest holds for a unit of a section included in the loop (only the identifiers); the cost of the second query; whether a variation registered in the editor (JavaScript) is worth having, since the marker is in the attribute.

#### Experiment of 2026-10-25: how heavy is merging all the HTML before the pagination (option 4)

Eric asked to check that the option is too heavy. **Measured with synthetic pages, in Chromium (Playwright `page.pdf`), A5, 5 JPEG photos of 1200 x 800 (about 170 KB each) and some text per unit, one unit per page; local images, no JavaScript, no web fonts, no map, no chart. It says little about the real pages of the blog, which have all of those.**

| Units | One document: time | peak memory of Chromium | PDF |
|---|---|---|---|
| 214 (the first book) | 5 s | 1.1 GB | 183 MB |
| 1000 | 32 s | 3.8 GB | 856 MB |

The same 214 units rendered **one by one** (a page each, one at a time): 17 s in all, the PDFs adding up to 186 MB (the same weight: the images are in the PDF either way).

**What this shows**: the volume alone does not make the option impossible. The memory grows with the number of units (about 1 to 4 MB per unit with this content) and a first book of about 200 units asks about 1 GB for the browser, which a small container would refuse and a normal one would not. Nothing slower than the per-unit way on this synthetic case.

**What it does not show, and where the real weight is likely**
- **WordPress has to render the whole section in one request**: 200 posts with their shortcodes, galleries, GPX maps and charts, against the memory limit and the time limit of PHP (30 s by default). Not measured: the pages of the blog are not reachable from here.
- **All the JavaScript of 200 posts in one page** (maps, charts, QR codes): duplicated element ids, scripts that assume one map per page, remote tiles asked all at once, and a wait for the end of all of them (the server of the PDF has a timeout; we set 300 s). Not measured.
- **One failure loses everything**, and there is **no cache per unit**: correcting one day renders the whole book again, where the per-unit way renders one unit and merges (the cache key of slice 220 is per post).
- The numbering and the table of contents are not simpler: the merge of the PDFs gives the page numbers just as well.

**Conclusion (Claude; noted by Eric on 2026-10-25 as the conclusion to keep for now, to be checked on the real pages)**: the argument against merging all the HTML is **not the volume** (a first book fits on a normal machine), it is the **robustness and the cost of a correction**: no cache per unit, one request to WordPress for the whole book, and all the JavaScript of the blog in one page. The founding decision stays: **one PDF per post, then merged**; option 4 is not retained. That is not proven either, since the real pages were not tried. If it ever has to be checked, the test is: the real section of 20 then 100 days, on the real site, through Gotenberg, watching the memory of PHP and of Chromium.

#### Series of 2026-10-26 on testing, with the real pages of the first book (option 4: merge all the HTML before the pagination)

Eric ran the branch `merge-all-html` on testing (the pages "journée" of last year's book, as a section; Gotenberg in the same Docker network, its `--api-timeout` at 300 s, the site asked by its internal address, slice 225; the PDF written to disk and sent as a file, slice 226). Times in seconds, from `merge.log` (`merge_done`, `pdf_convert`).

| Units | PHP renders the page | Whole conversion | PDF | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.4 | 1.7 | 2 MB | |
| 10 | 5.4 | 9.1 | 17.6 MB | |
| 20 | 8.0 | 18.0 | 34.6 MB | |
| 50 | 29.6 | 45.3 | 85.8 MB | first pass: the images of the print sizes were being made |
| 100 | 37.7 | 70.3 | 166.6 MB | PHP of the job 34 MB |
| **184** | **71.8** | **174.4** | **292 MB** | PHP of the page 108 MB, of the job 8 MB; 2982 queries |

**What it shows**
- **The cost is linear on the PHP side**: about 0.39 s and 16 SQL queries per unit, 0.58 MB of PHP memory per unit (108 MB for 184 units). The units that were slow (5 to 6 s, from the 87th on) were **the first rendering of units whose print-size images had not been made yet** (55 queries instead of 15, and a jump of the resident memory): once made, they cost 0.4 s. Not the proxy and not a plugin.
- **The print server is not linear**: Chromium and Gotenberg need 3.7 s for 10 units, 33 s for 100 and **103 s for 184** (0.33 s per unit, then 0.56). A book of 214 units would be about 85 s of PHP and about 130 s of Chromium, **about 215 s** in all, which is under the 300 s of `--api-timeout` but not by a wide margin; a book of 300 pages would have to raise it.
- **The weight is 1.6 MB per unit** (292 MB for 184), the same as the PDFs made unit by unit (the images are the same). Not tried: the files of the units together, to compare the sum.
- **Time went where it should not**: the first versions failed on the proxy (a limit of 60 s on a page that PHP needed 98 s to make, a `504`), on the memory of the job (496 MB of PHP for a PDF of 167 MB, because it was held in memory and sent as base64) and on the display (a JSON of 222 MB). Slices 225 and 226 removed those three; what remains is the time of PHP and of Chromium.
- **Not measured on these pages**: the memory of Gotenberg during the 184 units (about 720 MiB for 10 units), JavaScript heavier than the daily pages (the two summary pages of the book have whoBIRD thumbnails that do not all load, and a template that was never tried in the print mode), and a section of units that are sections.

**Conclusion, revised (Claude; to confirm by Eric)**: the argument of 2026-10-25 against merging all the HTML ("the volume") is **not confirmed for a book of daily pages**: 184 units take 3 minutes and a normal machine, once the three obstacles above are removed. What remains of the objection is not volume: **no cache per unit** (a correction in one day renders the whole book again, about 3 minutes, where the per-unit way renders one unit and merges), **all or nothing** (one failing unit, one page that does not load, and the whole conversion fails), **the pages that are not daily pages** (JavaScript, the templates that do not exist yet), and **the margin on the time of the print server** for a longer book. So the founding decision (one PDF per post, merged) is not overturned by a measure; option 4 becomes a **possible alternative for the book of daily pages**, to compare with the per-unit way **on the same pages** (time of the whole, time of a correction, failures) before deciding.


**Condition added by Eric (2026-10-26, to keep)**: merging the HTML is a possible alternative **provided the weight of every page is optimised as early as possible**, as was done for the daily pages (print-size images made once, light templates). The measure above holds only because of that work.

#### Page numbers, table of contents and index with a merged HTML, and markers in the PDF (Proposed; checked only in Chromium, 2026-10-26)

**What Chromium puts in a PDF** (checked with Playwright Chromium and `pypdf` on a document of 4 units, one of them over 3 pages; **not yet tried through Gotenberg**, nor after `gs`):
- an internal link (`<a href="#u-3">`) and an external marker link (`https://marker.invalid/unit/3`), even of one pixel and transparent, become `/Link` annotations carried by the page where they sit; the page of the annotation is the page where the unit starts;
- every `id` targeted by an internal link becomes a named destination (`/u-3` gives its page);
- `outline: true` also gives bookmarks (Gotenberg option to check);
- the number of pages of a unit is the difference between two consecutive starts; blank pages are counted for free.

**Proposed**: a marker link per unit and an `id` per passage to index, then a small reader of the page tree and of the annotations (the PDFs of Chromium have no object streams, which is what `PdfPages` already relies on; no `shell_exec`), which gives the page of each unit and of each passage. Markers are safer in the merged flow, because merging per-unit PDFs may rewrite the destinations; with per-unit PDFs the cumulative sum of `PdfPages::count()` is enough for the starts, and only the passages of the index need markers.

**Page numbers** (Proposed): a merged body is one document, so the physical pages are exact. The ToC and the index are small separate PDFs placed in front and at the end; their length does not depend on the digits, so the body is **not** rendered again. The folios are stamped on the final PDF (pdfcpu), which is the last pass, cheap because nothing is rendered.

**Open**: ToC and index as **posts with a role** ("ToC", "Index"), generated from the content, placed where wanted and styled by templates of the theme like the category pages. Their numbers come from the pagination map of the first pass, so in print they need one more small render (see the answer of the session); in web mode they carry links only.

**The margin of the printer (Eric and Claude, 2026-10-26; proposed)**: the "2 cm all around" of the CoolLibri FAQ is a safety recommendation for every format, not a measured limit (the printer does not check the layout); Eric's folios of last year were 1 cm from the bottom in A5 and were printed. Margins, gutter, bleed and the position of the folios are therefore settings of the printer preset ([slice 228](../slices/228-books-role-posts.md)), and Books should give the means to check them, with the weight and the resolution of pages and images ([slice 229](../slices/229-books-print-checks.md)).
